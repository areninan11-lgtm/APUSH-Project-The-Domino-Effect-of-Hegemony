APUSH Final Project · Developing Empathy in American History

The Domino
Effect of
Hegemony

How U.S. sanctions weaponized Cuba's colonial hierarchies and shaped modern Cuban displacement all the way from Havana to Ridgefield, New Jersey

Thesis: United States sanctions and imperialist policies, from the 1901 Platt Amendment through the 1962 embargo to today's maximum-pressure campaign, did not merely punish a government. They exploited pre-existing Spanish colonial hierarchies, trapping the most vulnerable Cubans in a cycle of economic warfare that still plays out in Hialeah, Florida and Ridgefield, New Jersey.

Slide 01 · Historical Origins

The Original Sin:
Cuba's Colonial Hierarchy

Spanish Colonial Era · Pre-1898

Before any American boot landed on Cuban soil, a rigid racial and social order (the casta system) had already defined who mattered and who did not. The hierarchy was not accidental; it was engineered by Spain to maximize extraction of sugar, tobacco, and labor.

"Cuba is the pearl of the Antilles, and it must remain so for Spain — its revenues, its sugar, its tobacco, are the lifeblood of the Crown." — Spanish colonial administrator, late 19th century (representative of peninsulare ideology documented in Cuban colonial records)
Speaker Notes — Slide 01

Opening: Before we can understand what America did to Cuba, we have to understand what Cuba already was. The casta system is the foundation every subsequent power — including the United States — would exploit.

Walk through the hierarchy. Afro-Cubans and enslaved people were at the very bottom, doing the labor that made Cuba profitable. The profits went to Spain. The structure was designed for extraction, not justice.

Key connection: When Cubans fought for independence in the Ten Years' War (1868–1878) and then the final war (1895–1898), Afro-Cubans made up the majority of the rebel armies. The revolution was multiracial. Then America arrived.

Transition: Cuba was on the verge of winning a freedom that would have looked very different from the racial and colonial order Spain had built. Then someone else arrived.

The Platt Amendment (1901) — Primary Source

The U.S. forced Cuba to embed the Platt Amendment directly into the Cuban Constitution as a condition of ending military occupation. Article III granted the United States the right to intervene militarily at any time to maintain Cuban "stability." Cuba was sovereign in name only.

Speaker Notes — Slide 02

The Flip: Your APUSH textbook probably showed you "School Begins" as just a cartoon about imperialism. Today I want to see it differently — as a document of arrogance. Look at what it's saying: Cuba is a child that needs to be taught. By a country that arrived after Cuba had been fighting for independence for 30 years.

On Máximo Gómez: He spent his life fighting for Cuba. When Havana was finally liberated, he wasn't allowed in the victory parade. American soldiers marched. Cuban soldiers were told to stand aside.

On the Platt Amendment: Read Article III: the U.S. can intervene in Cuba whenever it decides Cuba's government isn't stable enough. Cuba had a legal clause allowing foreign military occupation written into its own constitution. That's not independence.

Slide 03 · Economic Imperialism

Weaponizing the Economy:
From Land Grab to Blockade

Cold War Era · 1953–1996
The American Policy Timeline
1898–1958
Corporate Takeover
U.S. corporations acquire 75% of Cuba's agricultural land. United Fruit, Standard Oil dominate. Cuba becomes a monoculture sugar economy. Profits flow to Wall Street, not Havana.
1960
Eisenhower Embargo
Eisenhower implements trade embargo following Castro's nationalization of U.S. properties. Cold War framing: communist threat, not sovereign rights.
1961
Bay of Pigs
CIA-trained Cuban exiles attempt invasion. Fails. Deepens Castro's hold on power and justifies the regime's framing of the U.S. as Cuba's existential enemy.
1962
Kennedy Full Embargo — El Bloqueo Begins
Kennedy tightens to near-total embargo following Cuban Missile Crisis. Now 60+ years old.
1996
Helms-Burton Act
Codifies the embargo into law. Makes it extremely difficult for any future president to lift sanctions without an act of Congress. A legal lock on Cuba's economic strangulation.

Speaker Notes — Slide 03

The Key Argument: The U.S. liked to say the embargo was about democracy and human rights. But we had just helped overthrow a democracy in Guatemala (1954), propped up Batista's brutal dictatorship in Cuba until 1959, and supported authoritarian regimes across Latin America. This was never about democracy. It was about economic control.

On the word — 'Bloqueo' vs 'Embargo': The word matters. An embargo sounds distant and bureaucratic. A blockade is military language. It evokes a siege. Cubans didn't choose that word by accident.

The Hierarchy Connection: The embargo hurts ordinary Cubans most. Those with relatives in the U.S. can receive remittances. Those without — often Afro-Cubans — face the worst food and medicine shortages. The hierarchy reproduced itself through economic warfare.

Slide 05 · The Mutated Hierarchy

The Hierarchy That Never Died:
From Casta to Sanctions

Connecting Across Time

The Spanish casta system officially ended with Cuban independence. But the structure of who has power, who suffers, who is forgotten mutated and reproduced itself through every successive era. This is the core argument of historical empathy: the past is not past.

Cuban Missile Crisis Cartoon — 1962
Original Political Cartoon Gallery · Click to enlarge
Cuban Missile Crisis — Context Matters

This cartoon frames Cuba as the aggressor in 1962. The context it erases: the Bay of Pigs invasion (1961), CIA assassination plots against Castro, and a decade of economic warfare that drove Cuba to seek Soviet protection. The Missile Crisis was the result of U.S. aggression as much as Soviet expansion, but American cartoons told only one side. The full Kennedy embargo was signed directly after this crisis, codifying 60+ years of collective punishment.

Spanish Era · Pre-1898
Who held power?

Peninsulares were born in Spain, and governed Cuba from above. Extracted wealth. Controlled law.

Who suffered most?

Enslaved Africans, indigenous peoples. Their labor built the economy that enriched everyone above them.

American Imperial Era · 1898–1959
Who held power?

U.S. corporations and American-backed Cuban elites. The peninsulares were replaced by Wall Street.

Who suffered most?

Afro-Cuban independence fighters excluded from victory. Jim Crow-style segregation imported from the American South. Black Cubans pushed further to the margins even after bleeding most for independence.

Sanction Era · 1962–Present
Who holds power?

Cuban Communist Party leadership immune to sanctions, controls state resources. In Washington: politicians who haven't updated Cuba policy in 60 years.

Who suffers most?

Afro-Cubans were least likely to have family abroad sending remittances due to historical migration patterns. Worst food insecurity. Longest blackouts. Forced to flee.

Speaker Notes — Slide 05

Core Argument: This slide is the conceptual heart of the project. The same hierarchy — who suffers, who escapes, who has power — reproduced itself across 125 years, just wearing different clothes.

On the Interactive Quiz: Sanctions don't hurt governments — governments find workarounds. Sanctions hurt civilians. And within those civilians, historical patterns of inequality determine who suffers worst.

Historical Empathy Call: To understand Cuba today, you cannot just look at the news. You have to follow the hierarchy all the way back to the 17th century. The roots of modern Cuban suffering are 400 years deep.

Anthony's Story

My friend Anthony, who I met here in New Jersey in 4th grade, was part of a Cuban family that made it to the U.S. after years of navigating the wreckage of the Cuban economy. I didn't fully understand what his family had left behind until I learned: his family in Havana was dealing with blackouts that lasted 16 hours a day. There was no reliable access to medicine. Cooking oil, a basic staple, was rationed.

In 8th grade, Anthony had to move back to Florida to help support his family financially. Not because he wanted to leave. Because survival required it. That is what sanctions feel like from the inside. It is not a foreign policy debate, but a kid changing schools so his family can pay for food.

What This Means for Empathy
"Historical empathy is the process of understanding people in the past by contextualizing their actions." — Course definition, Developing Empathy in American History (Mr. Conry, 2026)

Anthony isn't a historical figure. He's a teenager. But his story is made possible only by history. By the Platt Amendment, by the embargo, and by decisions made in Washington D.C. decades before he was born. Historical empathy isn't just about the past. It's about seeing the people around you and understanding the forces that shaped their lives.

Why I Selected This Topic

I am not Cuban. I did not experience the embargo. But I grew up alongside friends like Anthony whose families did. I have seen what it means to leave a country with nothing. It is something that happens not because you want to, but because the political and economic forces around you have made staying impossible. I chose this topic because the narrative around Cuba is often polarized by Cold War rhetoric that erases the actual human beings in the middle. I want my classmates to see those people. In my opinion: the embargo should be substantially reformed to allow free trade in food, medical supplies, and humanitarian goods. Economic strangulation has not produced democracy in Cuba. It has produced suffering among ordinary Cubans while strengthening the government's narrative that America is the enemy.

Speaker Notes — Slide 06

Personal + Historical: This is the slide where I step out from behind the historical sources and speak directly. I am not Cuban. But I grew up next to this history. Empathy doesn't require you to have experienced something — it requires you to listen to those who have.

On Anthony: When Anthony had to move in 8th grade, I didn't fully understand why. I just knew he was gone. It took years of learning to connect what I was studying in history books to what was happening in my own neighborhood.

On your opinion: I believe the embargo should be reformed — not because I support the Cuban government, but because 60 years of evidence shows the embargo hasn't changed the Cuban government's behavior. It has only made the lives of ordinary Cubans harder.

Slide 07 · Cultivating Historical Empathy

Cultivating Historical Empathy:
A Call to Action

Now What?

Historical empathy is not passive. It requires active choices. Here is what you can do not as a future politician, not as a foreign policy expert, but as a person who now knows more than you did an hour ago.

📖
Read Primary Sources
Seek out the voices of Cubans themselves. Start with "Patria y Vida." Read Máximo Gómez's diary. The voices are there; they're just not in most textbooks.
🗣
Ask Before You Assume
If you know someone from Cuba, ask about their experience. Not "what do you think of your government?" but "what was your daily life like? What did you leave behind?"
🔍
Question the Framing
When you hear "sanctions" or "embargo," ask: who is this actually hurting? When you see a cartoon about Cuba, ask: whose perspective is missing from the frame?
🌐
Follow WOLA and HRW
Washington Office on Latin America (WOLA) publishes accessible Cuba policy analyses. Human Rights Watch documents both Cuban government abuses AND the humanitarian effects of sanctions. Both truths matter.
🗣
Connect to Civic Action
Cuba policy is made by elected officials. Understanding the human cost of these decisions is the first step toward holding representatives accountable for consequences, not just intentions.
🤝
Practice Cross-Cultural Empathy Now
Your classmates carry histories. Some were shaped by decisions the U.S. government made before any of you were born. Historical empathy starts with that recognition.
If You Live in New Jersey — Ways to Make Change

New Jersey has one of the largest Cuban-American and Latin American communities on the East Coast. Ridgefield, Weehawken, Union City, and North Bergen have deep Cuban and Cuban-American roots. Local action here carries real weight.

🏛
Write to Your U.S. Representatives
New Jersey's U.S. Senators and Representatives vote on legislation that affects Cuba policy. A constituent letter — especially from a student — gets read. Ask them where they stand on allowing humanitarian trade with Cuba: food, medicine, medical equipment.
Find your NJ rep → congress.gov
🎓
Bring This to Your School
Propose a panel, documentary screening, or cultural event at your school. Organizations like WOLA offer free educational materials. A conversation in a New Jersey classroom about the human cost of Cuba sanctions is a form of civic action.
WOLA Cuba resources → wola.org/cuba
📰
Write an Op-Ed or Letter to the Editor
The Jersey Journal, NJ.com, and local Bergen County papers publish letters from readers. Personal testimony from a New Jersey student carries more weight than another think-tank analysis.
NJ.com Letters → nj.com/letters
🇺
Connect with the Cuban Community in NJ
Union City, 3 miles from Ridgefield, is sometimes called "Havana on the Hudson." It has one of the densest Cuban-American populations in the U.S. outside Miami. Cuban cultural organizations there hold events and share resources for recent arrivals.
Union City NJ → unioncitynj.org
📱
Amplify Cuban Voices Online
Share the work of artists like the San Isidro Movement. Share journalism from outlets covering Cuba from the inside — 14ymedio (founded by Yoani Sánchez), CiberCuba, and El Toque. Amplification is political action — it breaks the information blockade that isolates Cuban voices.
14ymedio English → 14ymedio.com/en
💰
Support Organizations Directly Helping Cubans
Cuba Archive documents human rights abuses. CubaNow advocates for policy reform that helps ordinary Cubans. Puentes de Amor supports people-to-people connection. Even $10 sent to a humanitarian organization matters more than another geopolitical debate.
CubaNow → cubanow.org

They are human beings trapped between two unyielding political forces."

Speaker Notes — Slide 07

Closing energy: We started 125 years ago with a stolen revolution. We ended moments ago with Anthony having to leave New Jersey in 8th grade so his family could survive. That line — from 1898 to right now — is what historical empathy reveals.

On the NJ section: Union City, 3 miles away, is one of the densest Cuban communities in America outside Miami. This isn't a foreign issue. It's a neighborhood issue. The history is not over there — it's right here.

Final ask: I'm not asking you to have a specific political position on Cuba. I'm asking you to see Cubans as people, not as props in a Cold War argument.
